Chapter 1 
THE PROBLEM 
Introduction 
Education systems change as the needs of work and society change. Many countries 
have improved their basic education so that students are ready for college and employment. 
Career guidance supports this goal by helping students understand their skills and interests 
and the jobs available to them, so they can prepare for their future (OECD, 2023). In the 
Philippines, the K to 12 Program under Republic Act No. 10533 added Senior High School 
to prepare students for college, work, or business (Republic of the Ph ilippines, 2013). 
Students choose among tracks such as Academic, Technical -Vocational-Livelihood, Arts 
and Design, or Sports. Even with these options, many students still find it hard to pick the 
college course and career that suit them (Dayag & Picajas, 2025). 
One major reason is the lack of personalized career guidance that students can 
easily reach (Khurumova & Pinto, 2023). Schools usually give general advice through 
short sessions or talks with classmates. Counselors handle many students at once, so 
detailed support is hard to give. Interviews with thirty teachers and guidance designates 
from the participating schools showed the same picture. Career guidance was done 
manually through orientations, career talks, and individual counseling, most materials were 
on paper, and guidance duties were often given to a classroom teacher on top of a full 
teaching load. Technology can help by providing digital tools for counseling and career 
planning (OECD, 2023; Khurumova & Pinto, 2023). A web -based system can collect 
information from students and generate recommendations that support their decisions 
\n=== PDF PAGE 3 ===\n2 
 
(Bambi et al., 2023). The results can also serve as a reference during guidance activities. 
However, many of the online career tools reviewed for this study were not made for the 
Philippine Senior High School system and did not consider the K to 12 tracks. This shows 
the need for a local digital system that gives clear and personalized guidance to Senior 
High School students. 
This study developed Career Compass, a web -based decision support system that 
helps Senior High School students explore college courses and career paths (Bambi et al., 
2023). The system gathers the interests, skills, and academic performance of the student  
and ranks college courses using a rule -based weighted scoring method. Guidance 
counselors may use the printed results as a supporting reference during counseling. The 
study covered the design, development, and testing of the system. 
The study falls under the university thematic area of Industry, Energy, and 
Emerging Technology. It used web -based tools to address a problem in career guidance 
among Senior High School students. By applying information systems to education, the 
study provides a digital tool that may support guidance services in schools and benefit the 
community. 
The study also supports Sustainable Development Goals 4 and 8. Goal 4 promotes 
quality education and lifelong learning, and the system contributes by making career 
guidance easier to reach. Goal 8 promotes decent work and economic growth, and the 
system contributes by helping students plan for employment and prepare for the demands 
of the workforce. 
\n=== PDF PAGE 4 ===\n3 
 
Senior High School students often struggle to select suitable college courses and 
career paths because of limited guidance support. Most guidance is still done manually and 
in short counseling sessions that may not reach every student. This can lead to poor choices, 
low confidence, and difficulty in planning. A web -based system can generate course and 
career recommendations from an assessment and support counselors in their guidance 
work. It can also help students choose their future careers in a better informed way. 
Many of the existing career guidance tools give general advice and do not follow 
the Philippine Senior High School system. They often lack local data and are not connected 
to the career guidance programs of schools. The curriculum is also changing, since t he 
previous strand-based curriculum and the strengthened curriculum with elective subjects 
now exist side by side. Among the studies reviewed, none was found to apply a rule-based 
weighted scoring method that covers both the previous and Strengthened Senio r High 
School curricula. This is the gap that the study addressed. Career Compass links the local 
information, the student profile, and automated recommendations in one system so that 
students can choose their future careers with more confidence. 
The central argument of the study is that a web-based decision support system can 
support career guidance for Senior High School students by giving organized, 
personalized, and data -based recommendations. Career Compass was developed to help 
students select suitable college courses and career paths, and to give counselors a result 
they can refer to during guidance activities. 
Students in Senior High School benefit from the developed system through the 
assessment results and the ranked course and career recommendations based on their 
\n=== PDF PAGE 5 ===\n4 
 
answers. Guidance counselors benefit from the generated results, which serve as a 
supporting reference in counseling. Schools can use the system to strengthen their career 
guidance programs and the readiness of their students for further education and 
employment. Researchers and system developers can use the results and discussion of this 
study as a reference for similar educational support systems. 
Objectives of the Study 
The aim of the study is to develop a web -based decision support system to assist 
Senior High School students in selecting appropriate college courses and career paths. 
Specifically, the study aims to: 
1. Identify the data requirements needed as input of the system; 
2. Design and develop a web-based decision support system that applies the rule -based 
weighted scoring method in generating course and career recommendations; and 
3. Evaluate the system using the software quality standards of ISO/IEC 25010 in terms 
of functional suitability, reliability, and interaction capability. 
Scope and Limitations 
The study covered the development of Career Compass, a web -based decision 
support system for Senior High School career guidance. The system gives course and 
career recommendations based on the student’s interests, skills, and academic performance 
by applying a rule-based weighted scoring method to the assessment responses. It has two 
assessment flows, one for students under the previous strand-based curriculum and one for 
\n=== PDF PAGE 6 ===\n5 
 
students under the strengthened curriculum with elective subjects. Both flows use one 
shared list of 85 college courses. The assessment has 8 interest scenarios, 15 skills 
statements, and 4 academic steps. The results show the top five matches of the student and 
let the student explore all 85 courses through search, filters, and a comparison option. A 
short PDF report can be downloaded or printed for use during counseling sessions. The 
system is available in English and Filipino and runs in a web browser on  a desktop 
computer, laptop, or mobile device. Offline Mode can be turned on so the system keeps 
working on the same device without an internet connection. The assessment items and 
guidance materials were based on references from the Department of Educatio n and the 
guidance offices of Balogo High School, Ponso National High School, and Barayong 
National High School. The study included the planning, design, development, and testing 
of the system. The system was evaluated by selected guidance counselors and t eachers 
from the same three schools using ISO/IEC 25010. The assessment progress is stored in 
the browser’s localStorage on the user’s device. The system does not require account 
registration or login and does not use a database to store assessment responses. 
The study has several limitations. The system uses only the rule -based weighted 
scoring method and does not use artificial intelligence or machine learning. The 
recommendations depend on how complete and accurate the answers of the student are, so 
incomplete or inaccurate answers may lead to unsuitable results. The system covers only 
the 85 courses in its master list and does not include every course offered in the Philippines. 
An internet connection is needed to open the system for the first time and to turn on Offline 
Mode. Progress saved in the browser’s localStorage may remain available after the browser 
is closed. However, saved progress may be lost if the user clears the browser’s site data or 
\n=== PDF PAGE 7 ===\n6 
 
uses a different browser or device. Students may download or print the PDF report to keep 
a separate copy of their recommendations. The recommendations are supporting references 
only and are not final career decisions. The final decision remains with the student and the 
guidance counselor. The evaluation was limited to selected guidance counselors and 
teachers from the three schools, so the results do not represent other schools or locations. 
Students did not take part in the evaluation. 
Significance of the Study 
This study is important because it produced a web -based decision support system 
that may improve career guidance for Senior High School students. The recommendations 
are based on the interests, skills, and academic performance of the student. The following  
groups may benefit from the developed system: 
Senior High School Students. The assessment results and recommendations help 
students understand their strengths and the college courses and career paths open to them. 
The results can be used during counseling to talk about their options. This can reduce 
confusion and help students feel more certain about their decisions. 
Parents and Guardians.  The results help parents understand the career path 
suggested for their children. They can support their children’s decisions with better 
information and talk about these choices at home. 
Guidance Counselors. The system supports the guidance counselors of Balogo 
High School, Ponso National High School, and Barayong National High School in their 
\n=== PDF PAGE 8 ===\n7 
 
career guidance work. It provides organized and automated recommendations that can 
guide the counseling session. 
School. The digital assessment and recommendation tool can improve the career 
guidance services the school offers to its students. 
Educational Institutions.  The system may help schools improve their career 
guidance services and relate the Senior High School strands and electives to college 
programs. It may also support the preparation of students for further studies and 
employment. 
Researchers. The researchers gained experience in planning, designing, 
developing, and evaluating a web-based system using the ISO/IEC 25010 standard, which 
strengthened their technical and research skills. 
Future Researchers. Future researchers may use this study as a reference for 
similar systems. They may improve or expand the system by adding features or methods, 
and the results can guide further work in educational technology.

Chapter 2 
RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES 
This chapter presents the related literature and studies, project synthesis, conceptual 
framework, and definition of terms. 
Related Literature 
Bucad (2024) discussed a career path recommendation system for Senior High 
School students in selected public schools in the Philippines. The system used multi-label 
classification and considered students’ choices, backgrounds, and academic performance 
in recom mending suitable SHS tracks. The study emphasized the importance of clear 
criteria in matching students with possible educational paths. This literature supports the 
use of structured criteria in developing career recommendation systems. It is relevant to 
Career Compass because the system also considers student information when generating 
recommendations, although it does so through a rule-based weighted scoring method. 
Gunwant et al. (2022) reviewed twenty-five (25) studies on expert systems used for 
student career guidance. The review discussed different approaches, including rule-based, 
case-based, and fuzzy logic systems. It showed how expert systems can support stude nts 
in exploring educational and career options. This literature is relevant to Career Compass 
because it provides a basis for considering a rule-based approach that uses defined criteria 
to generate understandable course and career recommendations. 
The Organisation for Economic Co -operation and Development (OECD, 2023) 
discussed the role of digital technologies in supporting educational services and access to 
 
\n=== PDF PAGE 11 ===\n10 
 
information. Digital tools can help educational institutions provide information and support 
students during their learning experiences. This literature is relevant to Career Compass 
because the system uses a web -based platform to make career guidance info rmation and 
assessment-based course recommendations more accessible to Senior High School 
students. 
Bambi et al. (2023) discussed the use of Decision Support Systems (DSS) in 
education. A DSS supports decision -making by organizing relevant information and 
applying established procedures or rules. This approach is useful when decisions require 
consideration of several factors. The literature is relevant to Career Compass because the 
system uses a rule-based weighted scoring method to process students’ interests, skills, and 
academic performance and generate possible college course recommendations. 
The Department of Education (DepEd, 2025) introduced the pilot implementation 
of the Strengthened Senior High School (SHS) Curriculum for Grade 11 in School Year 
2025–2026. The curriculum reduced the previous four SHS tracks to two: the Academic 
Track and the Technical -Professional (TechPro ) Track. Following the pilot 
implementation, DepEd (2026) announced the full implementation of the Strengthened 
SHS Curriculum for Grade 11 beginning School Year 2026–2027, while Grade 12 learners 
in non-pilot schools continued under the previous curriculum. These changes are relevant 
to Career Compass because the system supports students under both the previous and 
Strengthened SHS curricula. The system considers differences in strands, subjects, and 
electives when generating course and career recommendations.

Related Studies 
Bejar (2024) developed a career path selection system that used data mining 
techniques to assist incoming Senior High School students in exploring possible career 
options. The study is related to Career Compass because both aim  to support students in 
making educational and career decisions. However, Career Compass uses a rule -based 
weighted scoring method rather than a data mining approach. This allows the system to 
generate recommendations using predefined criteria without requ iring a large training 
dataset. 
Dayag and Picajas (2025) examined structured career guidance services for Grade 
11 students. Their study emphasized the importance of organized guidance activities in 
supporting students’ career readiness and decision-making. This study is relevant to Career 
Compass becaus e the system provides a structured assessment process and generates 
course and career recommendations that may serve as supporting references during career 
guidance activities. 
Atay et al. (2024) developed a web-based career counseling information system in 
Türkiye that provided access to career -related information and counseling resources. The 
study demonstrated the application of web technologies in supporting career guidance 
services. It is related to Career Compass because both systems use web-based platforms to 
provide career information. However, Career Compass focuses on generating college 
course recommendations based on students’ interests, skills, and academic performance  
within the Philippine Senior High School context. 
\n=== PDF PAGE 13 ===\n12 
 
Khurumova and Pinto (2023) examined students’ awareness and use of online and 
computer-assisted career guidance tools. Their study highlighted the importance of 
considering students’ readiness and willingness to use digital career guidance services. 
These findings are relevant to Career Compass because they emphasize the need for an 
accessible and understandable system. Career Compass incorporates a journey -themed 
assessment environment to encourage students to participate in the career exploration 
process. 
Villaruz and Diamante (2024) developed a web-based enrollment appraisal system 
using a rule -based Decision Support System. The system applied predefined rules to 
process student information and assess enrollment eligibility. The study also used the 
Rapid Application Development methodology and ISO/IEC 25010 for software quality 
evaluation. This study is relevant to Career Compass because both systems apply rule -
based decision-making in an educational setting and use a web -based platform to support 
school-related processes. 
Wang et al. (2025) introduced CareerPooler , an AI -powered career exploration 
system that used interactive simulations to support career exploration. The study is related 
to Career Compass because both systems incorporate interactive features to encourage 
users to explore possible career paths. How ever, Career Compass uses a rule -based 
weighted scoring method and a journey-themed assessment environment instead of an AI-
based recommendation approach. This allows the system to generate recommendations 
based on predefined course alignment rules.

Project Synthesis 
The reviewed literature and studies discussed the difficulties experienced by Senior 
High School students in choosing college courses and career paths. They also showed how 
Decision Support Systems can assist in organizing information and supporting decisi ons 
that involve several factors, such as interests, skills, and academic performance. Gunwant 
\n=== PDF PAGE 15 ===\n14 
 
et al. (2022) discussed the application of expert systems in student career guidance, while 
Villaruz and Diamante (2024) demonstrated the application of a rule -based Decision 
Support System in an educational setting. Bucad (2024) examined the use of studen t 
information in career path recommendations. Together, these studies provided a basis for 
selecting a rule -based weighted scoring method that uses student information for Career 
Compass. 
The reviewed studies presented different approaches to digital career guidance, 
including data mining, web -based counseling, and AI -powered career exploration. Bejar 
(2024) examined the use of data mining techniques, while Atay et al. (2024) developed a 
web-based career counseling information system. Khurumova  and Pinto (2023) 
highlighted the importance of students’ readiness to use online career guidance tools, while 
Wang et al. (2025) explored an interactive approach to career guidance. In the Philippines, 
the Department of Education (2025, 2026) introduced c hanges to the Senior High School 
curriculum, resulting in the transition from the previous four -track structure to the 
Strengthened SHS Curriculum. These developments emphasize the importance of 
providing a career guidance system that considers students’ e ducational backgrounds and 
supports both curriculum structures during the transition. 
Based on the reviewed literature and studies, several areas were identified for 
improvement in digital career guidance. Existing systems applied different approaches to 
career recommendations, including data mining, rule -based decision -making, and 
interactive career exploration. However, the reviewed studies provided limited discussion 
of a web -based career guidance system that combines a rule -based weighted scoring 
method, student interests, skills, academic performance, and support for both the previous 
\n=== PDF PAGE 16 ===\n15 
 
and Strengthened Philippine Senior High School curricula. These areas provided the basis 
for developing Career Compass as a system intended to support career guidance activities 
in the local educational setting. 
Career Compass was developed to address the identified needs through a web-based 
Decision Support System that uses a rule -based weighted scoring method. The system 
processes students’ interests, skills, and academic performance to generate college course 
and career recommendations. It supports both the previous and Strengthened Senior High 
School curricula and provides printable assessment results that may serve as supporting 
references during counseling activities. The system also includes a journey -themed 
assessment environment designed to make career exploration more engaging and 
organized. These features reflect the findings of the reviewed literature and studies while 
providing a system suited to the Philippine Senior High School context. 
Conceptual Framework 
The conceptual framework of this study applies the Input –Process–Output (IPO) 
model, as presented in Figure 1. It illustrates the inputs, development processes, and output 
involved in the development of Career Compass: A Web-Based Decision Support System 
for Senior High School Career Guidance. 
Input. The input consists of three components: Data Requirements, Design 
Requirements, and Evaluation Requirements. Data Requirements include the information 
gathered through interviews and the review of related literature and studies, which served 
as the basis for identifying the system’s required information and functions. Design 
Requirements include the tools and technologies used to develop a web -based system 
accessible through desktop computers, laptops, and mobile devices. The system uses the 
\n=== PDF PAGE 17 ===\n16 
 
browser’s localStorage to save assessment progress on the user’s device without requiring 
account registration or centralized database storage. Evaluation Requirements include the 
criteria used to assess the system’s quality in terms of functional suitability, reliability, and 
interaction capability based on ISO/IEC 25010. 
Process. The process follows the Rapid Application Development (RAD) 
methodology, which consists of four phases: Requirements Planning, User Design, 
Construction, and Cutover. During Requirements Planning, the researchers gathered 
information about the sys tem requirements. During User Design, the system features and 
interface were planned and designed. During Construction, the researchers developed and 
refined the system. During Cutover, the developed system underwent testing and 
evaluation in preparation for its implementation. 
Output. The output was Career Compass: A Web-Based Decision Support System 
for Senior High School Career Guidance. The feedback loop links the output to the input. 
This means that the findings from the testing and evaluation were used to improve the 
system.

Career Guidance.  A service that helps students know themselves and the jobs 
available to them so they can make good decisions for their future (OECD, 2023). In this 
study, career guidance is the help the system gives students by showing possible courses 
and careers, together with the support that guidance counselors give through the system. 
Decision Support System (DSS).  A computer-based tool that helps people make 
decisions. It collects information, applies rules, and gives suggestions to the user (Bambi 
et al., 2023). In this study, the Decision Support System is the web -based platform that 
collects student information and generates course and career suggestions using the scoring 
method. 
Rule-Based Weighted Scoring Method. A decision-making approach that assigns 
points to different criteria according to predefined rules. In this study, the method is used 
to calculate and rank college courses based on students’ interests, skills, and academic 
information. The resulting rankings serve as recommendations for career exploration rather 
than final decisions about a student’s college course. 
Senior High School (SHS). Senior High School refers to Grades 11 and 12 of the 
Philippine basic education system under the K to 12 Program (Republic of the Philippines, 
2013). In this study, SHS refers to the educational level of the students for whom Career 
Compass was developed. The system supports both the previous and Strengthened SHS 
curricula when processing students’ academic information and generating college course 
recommendations. 
\n=== PDF PAGE 20 ===\n19 
 
Web-Based System.  An application that is accessed through a web browser 
without being installed on the device. It can be opened on the internet using desktop 
computers, laptops, or mobile devices (Atay et al., 2024). In this study, the web -based 
system refers to Career Comp ass, which is used to complete the career assessment and 
generate printable career recommendation results in a browser. 
Course Recommendation.  A suggested college course based on a person’s 
interests, skills, and assessment responses that helps in choosing a suitable course or career 
path. In this study, course recommendation refers to the recommended college courses and 
careers generated by proc essing the assessment responses using the rule -based weighted 
scoring method. 
Guidance Counselor. School designates who assist students in making educational 
and career-related decisions by providing guidance, counseling, and career support services 
(Dayag & Picajas, 2025). In this study, guidance counselor refers to the school designates 
who facilitate the use of Career Compass and use the generated results as a supporting 
reference during career counseling activities. 
ISO/IEC 25010.  An international standard that provides models for evaluating 
system and software product quality (International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 
2023). In this study, ISO/IEC 25010 was used as the basis for evaluating Career Compass 
in terms of functional suitability, reliability, and interaction capability. 
Rapid Application Development (RAD).  An approach to software development 
that works quickly and involves users throughout the process. Changes are made based on 
user feedback so that the final product meets the requirements of the users (Villaruz & 
Diamante, 2024). In this study, RAD refers t o the development method used to build the 
\n=== PDF PAGE 21 ===\n20 
 
system. It has four phases: Requirements Planning, User Design, Construction, and 
Cutover. The researchers gathered user feedback at each phase to improve the system. 
K to 12 Program. The education program in the Philippines that added two years 
to basic education, making it twelve years in total. It was established through Republic Act 
No. 10533 to help students prepare for college, work, or business (Republic of the 
Philippines, 2013). In this study, the K to 12 Program provides the academic tracks and 
career options used as references in building Career Compass. 
Local Storage. A browser-based mechanism that stores data on a user’s device and 
generally retains the stored information after the browser is closed, unless the data are 
cleared or removed. In this study, localStorage refers to the browser storage mechanism 
used by Care er Compass to save assessment progress and allow users to continue their 
assessment on the same browser and device without creating an account.

Chapter 3 
RESEARCH DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY 
This chapter describes the research methodology and approach used in developing 
the system. It includes the research method, sources of data, data gathering procedure, 
software development model, system requirements, evaluation tool, statistical tools, and  
ethical considerations of the study. 
Research Method 
This study developed Career Compass: A Web-Based Decision Support System for 
Senior High School Career Guidance. It used a descriptive–developmental research design. 
The descriptive part identified the career guidance practices and requirements of 
the participating schools. The data were gathered from teachers and guidance designates 
who handle career guidance for Senior High School students choosing college courses and 
career paths. The results showed what the system had to handle and what information it 
had to include. 
The developmental part guided the building of the system. The researchers 
designed and built the web-based system using the information gathered. The system gives 
course and career recommendations to Senior High School students and generates 
assessment res ults that can assist the guidance counselor during career counseling 
activities. 
 
 
\n=== PDF PAGE 26 ===\n25 
 
Sources of Data 
This study used two data sources to support the building and evaluation of Career 
Compass: primary data and secondary data. Together they ensured that the system was 
based on actual and current information and supported by existing knowledge in the field. 
The primary data came from individuals directly involved in career guidance at the 
Senior High School level. Thirty (30) teachers and guidance designates, ten (10) from each 
of Balogo High School, Ponso National High School, and Barayong National High School, 
served as the respondents of the study. They were selected through purposive sampling. 
The available Senior High School teachers and guidance designates who handle or take 
part in the career guidance process of their school were invited, and those who agreed 
became the respondents. The same thirty (30) respondents took part in both stages of the 
study. They were first interviewed to identify the current career guidance practices in their 
schools and the information the system had to handle. School docum ents such as career 
guidance forms and sample academic record forms were also reviewed to identify the 
specific data requirements of the system. Later, the same respondents used the system and 
went through the assessment flow before answering the evaluation questionnaire. Students 
were not participants in this study. Guidance counselors may use the system with students 
in future counseling sessions, but this is outside the scope of this study. 
The secondary data came from published materials related to the study, such as 
books, academic journals, related studies, and government publications. These covered 
career guidance, decision support systems, rule -based systems, the K to 12 Program, and 
software quality evaluation using ISO/IEC 25010. They supported the theoretical and 
\n=== PDF PAGE 27 ===\n26 
 
empirical basis for the design and development of Career Compass and the methods used. 
Course details, admission requirements, and related information from CHED memoranda, 
official school websites, and industry reports in the Philippines were used to build  the 
course profiles and the static dataset that supports the scoring system and the information 
shown to users. 
Data Gathering Procedure 
  The researchers carried out a two -stage data gathering process. Each stage had 
a specific purpose in supporting the development of Career Compass. 
The first stage was the Requirements and Data Collection Stage. The researchers 
sent a formal letter to each school to ask permission to conduct the study. Once approved, 
they interviewed the teachers and guidance designates about the current career guidan ce 
process in the school and the common problems students face when choosing college 
courses and career directions. The interviews used Instrument 1, the Interview Guide for 
Requirements Gathering, which contained open-ended questions that allowed participants 
to share their experiences and suggestions. School documents such as career guidance 
forms and career assessment forms were also used to identify the data requirements of the 
system. The three participating schools have functioning guidance offices an d internet-
accessible facilities suitable for system testing and evaluation. 
The second stage was the System Evaluation Stage. The developed system was 
presented to the guidance counselors and teachers for testing. They used the system directly 
and went through the assessment flow. After using it, they answered Instrument 2, the 
\n=== PDF PAGE 28 ===\n27 
 
Career Compass System Evaluation Questionnaire. The questionnaire used close -ended 
questions to gather their responses about the system. The responses were used to evaluate 
the system and to identify improvements. 
Software Developmental Model 
  The researchers used the Rapid Application Development (RAD) model in 
developing Career Compass. RAD is a software development approach that allows a 
system to be built in a short period of time while meeting the needs of its users (Pressman 
& Maxim, 2020). It also allows the developers to improve the system using feedback from 
users during development. Villaruz and Diamante (2024) also used RAD in developing a 
rule-based decision support system for a school process. The model has four phases, as 
shown in Figure 2. 
 
Figure 2. Rapid Application Development Model 
Requirements Planning Stage is the phase where the researchers determine the 
needs and the requirements of the system . In this phase, the researchers determined the 
needs and requirements of the system. They gathered information from the guidance 

\n=== PDF PAGE 29 ===\n28 
 
designates and teachers through interviews and a review of school documents. This 
information was used to decide the features and functions that Career Compass needed. 
User Design Stage is the phase where the researchers make the design of the system 
ready in accordance with the identified requirements.  In this phase, the researchers 
prepared the design of the system according to the identified requirements. They designed 
the layout, interface, and process flow of Career Compass and prepared the site map, 
wireframes, assessment items, and scoring rules. 
Construction Stage is the phase where the design that has been approved becomes 
a system. In this phase, the approved design was turned into a working system. The 
researchers built the main features of Career Compass and implemented the rule -based 
weighted scoring method that generates the course and career recommendations. 
Cutover Stages is the phase where the system is completed, tested, and ready for 
use. In this phase, the system was completed, tested, and made ready for use. The 
researchers tested the system, corrected the errors found, and made final adjustments to 
make sure it worked properly before the evaluation.

Evaluation Tool 
The developed system was evaluated using the ISO/IEC 25010 Software Quality 
Model. This model is used to check the quality of a software product and defines the quality 
characteristics to be measured (International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 
2023). The quality of a system depends on how well it meets the needs of its users and 
gives them value. Each quality characteristic has sub-characteristics that describe in more 
detail how quality is measured. 
This study used three quality characteristics, chosen according to the third objective 
of the study: functional suitability, reliability, and interaction capability. 
\n=== PDF PAGE 32 ===\n31

needs of its users. It examines whether the system has the needed features, gives correct 
results, and helps users complete their tasks (ISO, 2023). 
Reliability checks whether the system runs without errors and stays available when 
needed. It also examines whether the system can handle minor difficulties and recover from 
interruptions (ISO, 2023). 
Interactions Capability checks whether users can use the system to finish their tasks. 
It examines how easy the system is to understand, learn, and navigate (ISO, 2023).

Ethical Consideration 
This study observed proper ethical standards to protect the rights and welfare of all 
participants. The researchers followed the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 
10173), which requires that personal information be collected and used only for a declared 
\n=== PDF PAGE 36 ===\n35 
 
and legitimate purpose (Republic Act No. 10173, 2012). Before data collection, the 
researchers asked for written permission from the school administrations through a formal 
letter requesting approval for the interviews and the system evaluation. The purpose, scope, 
and procedures of the study were explained to the participants before the activities so that 
each of them understood what they were taking part in. 
Participation in the study was voluntary. The guidance counselors and teachers who 
were asked to take part could refuse, no one was forced to answer any question or complete 
any activity, and anyone could leave at any time without penalty. 
The identity of each participant was kept private. No full name or identification 
number was collected through the survey questionnaire or the system evaluation. All 
responses were treated as confidential and used only for the purpose of this study, and no  
single response was disclosed to anyone outside the research team. 
The gathered data were stored securely, and only the researchers and their adviser 
had access to the responses. The results are reported in summary form so that no individual 
participant can be identified. At the end of the study, all gathered data will be  properly 
disposed of to prevent unauthorized access. 
The Career Compass system itself was also designed with data privacy in mind. It 
uses the browser’s localStorage to save the assessment responses and progress of its users. 
This data stays on the user’s own device and is not sent to or saved on any external server 
or database. The data may remain on the device after the browser is closed until the user 
clears the browser’s site data. The system does not require an account, and the name 
\n=== PDF PAGE 37 ===\n36 
 
entered by the user is optional. This design keeps the responses of the users private and 
follows the principles of the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). 
The study did not require participants to answer questions that could cause 
emotional or psychological discomfort. The survey items were limited to evaluating the 
features and interaction capability of Career Compass, and no question asked for sensitive 
personal information beyond what the study needed.

DATA ANALYSIS AND INTERPRETATION 
 
 This chapter presents the data gathered, the development of the system, and the 
evaluation results. It follows the three objectives of the study. The first part shows the data 
requirements and the existing process. The second part describes the design, the  scoring 
rules, and the features of Career Compass. The third part presents the testing of the system 
and the evaluation results based on ISO/IEC 25010 (International Organization for 
Standardization [ISO], 2023). 
Data Requirements 
 To identify the data that the system must handle, the researchers interviewed thirty 
(30) teachers and guidance designates from Balogo High School, Ponso National High 
School, and Barayong National High School. The interview used Instrument 1, the 
Interview Guide for Requirements Gathering. It covered the current guidance process, the 
problems met, the materials used, and the features wanted in the system. The answers were 
grouped by theme and counted. One respondent could give more than one answer, so the 
counts do not add up to 30. Twenty -seven (27) respondents answered the question on 
desired features. 
 All 30 respondents described some form of structured career guidance, such as 
orientations, career talks, counseling, and assessments. The schedule was not the same 
across schools. Some respondents gave guidance almost daily, some weekly, some once or 
twice a year, and some only when needed. Fifteen respondents said guidance is part of their 
teaching or homeroom activities. Ten said a guidance coordinator works with advisers and 
 
\n=== PDF PAGE 40 ===\n39 
 
subject teachers. Seven said guidance is given on top of a regular teaching load. Most 
materials were on paper or in hand-entered spreadsheets, and no shared digital record was 
found in the three schools. 
 The respondents named several problems that students face. The most common 
were financial limits on course choice (25 of 30) and family influence on career decisions 
(18 of 30). Fifteen mentioned that students lack self -awareness or are undecided, and 15 
mentioned that students know little about the available courses and pathways. Job prospects 
and access problems such as distance and schedules were each mentioned by 11. Limited 
counselor time was mentioned by 9. These findings show that the system should give clear 
course information, including careers, schools, and salary estimates, and should help 
students see their own interests and strengths. The limited time of counselors shows the 
need for a result that can be used right away, such as a printable report. 
 For existing materials, 26 of 30 respondents collected demographic and academic 
data, and 22 used career or interest assessment tools. Nineteen collected career preferences, 
18 kept student profiles and academic records, and 14 collected interests, skills,  and 
strengths. Eight used counseling or referral forms. This shows that the input of the system 
should cover interests, skills, and academic performance. 
 For the desired features, 15 of 27 respondents wanted automated course and career 
recommendations. Twelve wanted digital student profiles and 12 wanted a central place 
for career information. Eleven wanted a simple interface, and 10 wanted an assessment 
with a generated result. Ten wanted data security, eight asked for access in low -
connectivity areas, and five wanted printable or exportable output.  Other requests were 
mentioned by fewer respondents. Eight wanted regular maintenance and updating of the 
\n=== PDF PAGE 41 ===\n40 
 
system, four wanted job market information, four wanted a way to track career plans over 
time, four wanted dashboards and summaries, two wanted non -degree pathways, two 
wanted links across school offices, and two suggested advanced personalization.  Because 
financial limits were the most common problem, the system should also show scholarship 
opportunities together with the course information, so students can see that support for 
college costs exists.

course and career recommendations; (3) give clear course information, including caree rs, 
schools, and salary estimates; (4) produce a printable result for counselors; (5) have a

Table 8 shows the scoring rules. The system uses a rule -based weighted scoring 
method. It has 8 interest questions, 15 skills statements, and the Valley section, which 
depends on the curriculum route. The Valley collects the strand and subjects (old route) or 
the electives and core subjects (new route), the general weighted average, and Certificates 
and Achievements. Each of the 85 courses is linked to the same number of items. Under 
both routes, each course is linked to 2 interest options, 2 skills, and 2 Certificates and 
Achievements categories. For the old route, each course is also linked to 1 strand and 2 
subjects, which gives 9 links. For the new route, each course is also linked to 1 core subject 
and 5 electives, which gives 12 links. Counting both ro utes, each course has 15 links, and 
\n=== PDF PAGE 52 ===\n51 
 
the 85 courses have 1,275 links in all, so no course has more chances to score than another. 
The total score of a course is the sum of the points from its links. A student in the GAS 
strand receives no strand points and no general weighted average bonus for any course, so 
the ranking for that student depends on the other items. 
Courses are ranked from the highest to the lowest total score. If two courses have 
the same total, the course with more interest points ranks higher. If they are still tied, skills 
points are compared, and then academic points from the strand, subjects, el ectives, core 
subject, and general weighted average. Points from Certificates and Achievements count 
in the total score but are not compared separately. Courses that are still tied after these 
checks get the same rank and are shown in alphabetical order by course name. Alphabetical

Chapter 5 
SUMMARY OF FINDINGS, CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
This chapter presents the summary of findings, the conclusions, and the 
recommendations of the study “Career Compass: A Web-Based Decision Support System 
for Senior High School Career Guidance.” 
Summary of Findings 
The study aimed to develop a web-based decision support system that helps Senior 
High School students choose suitable college courses and career paths. The system uses a 
rule-based weighted scoring method on the interests, skills, and academic performance of 
the student. It also gives guidance counselors a printed result that they can use as a 
supporting reference. Guided by the Rapid Application Development (RAD) model, the 
researchers identified the data requirements of the system, designed and developed Career 
Compass, and evaluated it using the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model. The findings 
are summarized according to the three specific objectives of the study. 
1. The interviews with 30 teachers and guidance designates from Balogo High 
School, Ponso National High School, and Barayong National High School showed the data 
and features the system needed. Career guidance in the three schools was done by hand 
through orientations, career talks, counseling, and assessments. It was often given by a 
classroom teacher on top of a full teaching load, the schedule was not the same across 
schools, and most materials were on paper. The most common problems of students were 
limited money (25 of 30), family influence (18 of 30), lack of self -awareness (15 of 30), 
and little knowledge of available courses (15 of 30). The most wanted feature was 
automated course and career recommendation (15 of 27), followed by digital student 
 
\n=== PDF PAGE 72 ===\n71 
 
profiles and a central place for career information (12 of 27 each) and a simple interface 
(11 of 27). From these findings, the researchers set eight requirements for the system. The 
system needed to collect interests, skills, and academic performance, giv e clear course 
information, and produce a printable result for counselors. 
2.  Career Compass was designed and developed using the Rapid Application 
Development model. The system has 19 features. These include a guided journey in three 
trails (Mountains, Forest, and Valley), a rule -based scoring engine, a top five result with 
all 85 courses to explore, a course detail page, course comparison, a printable PDF report, 
an English and Filipino language switch, progress saving in the browser’s localStorage, 
and an opt -in Offline Mode. The course detail page also shows scholarship informati on, 
which responds to limited money, the most common problem of students. The system 
supports both the old and the strengthened Senior High School curriculum. It scores each 
course from its links to interests, skills, and academic performance, which includ es 
Certificates and Achievements. The assessment items were validated by the three guidance 
designates of the participating schools. The highest possible score is 38 for the old route 
and 43 for the new route. Each of the 85 courses has the same number of links, so no course 
has more chances to score than another. The system has no account, no login, and no 
database, and the answers of the student stay on the device. The system components test 
showed that the assessment, scoring, results, and PDF report worked as expected. 
3.  The system was evaluated by 30 guidance counselors and teachers using 
ISO/IEC 25010. The overall weighted mean was 4.77, interpreted as Strongly Agree. 
Reliability had the highest weighted mean (4.83), followed by Functional Suitability (4.77) 
and Interaction Capability (4.73). All 18 indicators were rated Strongly Agree. The highest 
\n=== PDF PAGE 73 ===\n72 
 
indicator was Item 18 on clear buttons, labels, and options (5.00). The lowest was Item 11 
on whether the interface clearly shows that the system is for career guidance (4.53). The 
next lowest was Item 12 on ease of learning and use (4.63). Of the 540 ratings, 96.7% were 
Agree or Strongly Agree, and only three ratings were below 3. These results are in line 
with Dayag and Picajas (2025), who stressed that organized guidance supports the career 
readiness of students, since the evaluators found the structured assessment suitable for 
guidance tasks. Like Villaruz and Diamante (2024), this study evaluated a rule -based 
decision support system for a school process using ISO/IEC 25010. 
Conclusions 
The study was conducted for the purpose of developing Career Compass, a web -
based decision support system that supports Senior High School career guidance by 
generating course and career recommendations from students’ interests, skills, and 
academic perfor mance. In line with its objectives, the study arrived at the following 
conclusions: 
1. The data requirements were identified and matched the real needs of the schools. 
The interviews showed that guidance is done by hand, that teachers have little time for it, 
and that students need clear information about courses. They also showed that the input of 
the system should be the interests, skills, and academic performance of the student. The 
data requirements were therefore enough to guide the design of Career Compass. 
2. Career Compass was designed and developed as planned. It applies the rule -
based weighted scoring method, supports both curricula, and gives ranked course 
recommendations that the student and the guidance counselor can use as a supporting 
reference. The scoring rules are fixed, can be checked, and were validated by the guidance 
\n=== PDF PAGE 74 ===\n73 
 
designates of the three schools. The system works without an account or a database. This 
shows that a rule-based system can give organized and personal course recommendations. 
Unlike the data mining approach of Bejar (2024), the rule -based method of Career  
Compass does not need a large set of training data, which makes it easier to build and to 
check in a school setting. 
3.  Career Compass was rated Strongly Agree by the evaluators on the three 
ISO/IEC 25010 quality characteristics used in this study. The overall weighted mean was 
4.77 (Strongly Agree). By characteristic, Functional Suitability got 4.77, Reliability got 
4.83, and Interaction Capability got 4.73, all interpreted as Strongly Agree. The evaluators 
strongly agreed that the system has the needed functions, works reliably, and is easy to use. 
The results suggest that the system may serve as a supporting tool in the career guidance 
of Senior High School students. The lower ratings for Ite ms 11 and 12 show that the first 
screens can explain the purpose of the system more clearly. 
The conclusions are limited in several ways. The evaluators were teachers and 
guidance designates from three schools, and students did not take part, so the results do 
not show how students feel about the system. The tests check that the scoring follows the 
rules. They do not measure how accurate the recommendations are for real students. The 
system covers only 85 courses, and its recommendations depend on the answers of the 
student. The results are supporting references only, and the final decision stays w ith the 
student and the guidance counselor. 
 
 
 
\n=== PDF PAGE 75 ===\n74 
 
Recommendations 
In light of the findings and conclusions, the following recommendations are given 
to improve the system and to guide schools and future studies. The first four are for 
improving the system, and the last three are for schools and future researchers. 
1. Add more courses and update the course data every year. The system covers 85 
courses, and salary estimates, schools, requirements, and scholarship information change 
over time. Regular updates also answer the request of 8 of 27 respondents for regular 
maintenance. 
2.  Add non-degree and technical-vocational pathways, and connect the system to 
current job market data. The system already shows careers, salary estimates, schools, and 
scholarship information for each of the 85 courses. However, 2 of 27 respondents asked 
for non-degree pathways, and 4 of 27 asked for job market information. The system covers 
only college courses, and its salary data is fixed, so students cannot see other options or 
current job information. Limited money was the most common problem of students (25 of 
30), so showing low-cost pathways and current job information can help them plan. Current 
job data needs outside data sources, so this can be done together with Recommendation 3. 
3.  Add an optional account and a database. These were outside the approved scope, 
but 12 of 27 respondents wanted digital student profiles, and 4 of 27 wanted a way to track 
career plans over time. A database would let counselors keep results and follow up wi th 
students. It would also need strong data privacy protection under the Data Privacy Act of 
2012. 
\n=== PDF PAGE 76 ===\n75 
 
4.  Add a counselor dashboard and links with other school offices. Four respondents 
wanted dashboards and summaries, and two wanted links across offices. These features 
need a database and a login, so they can be done together with Recommendation 3. 
5.  Let guidance counselors use the system in their regular guidance activities, and 
train them to read the results. Limited counselor time was a problem in the schools (9 of 
30), and the printed report can save time if counselors know how to discuss it with students 
and their families. 
6.  Test the system with Senior High School students. Students did not take part in 
this evaluation. A study with students can show how they feel about the system, whether 
the recommendations match their later choices, and whether the first screens are clear t o 
them. This follows the lower ratings for Items 11 and 12. 
7.  Study other ways to add personalization, such as machine learning, in future work. 
Two respondents suggested advanced personalization. This study used only a rule -based 
method, so other methods can be compared with it using real student data.
